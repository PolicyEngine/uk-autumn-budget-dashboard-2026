1          Introduction


Background
1.1        The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) was established in 2010 to provide independent
           and authoritative analysis of the UK public finances. Alongside the UK Government's
           Budgets and other fiscal events, we publish forecasts for the economy and public finances in
           our Economic and fiscal outlook (EFO). Under the fiscal frameworks agreed between the UK
           Government and the Scottish and Welsh Governments, we are also responsible for
           producing the rest of UK forecasts of revenues and spending that underpin the block grant
           adjustments (BGAs) which determine the annual reduction in funding from the UK
           Government to reflect the devolution of tax powers.1 This document sets out those forecasts
           alongside the latest outlook for the devolved taxes. We also set out projections of the net tax
           positions in Scotland and Wales, which are the difference between the forecasts of devolved
           revenues and the BGAs.

1.2        The methodologies and the forecasts represent the collective view of the independent
           members of the OBR’s Budget Responsibility Committee (BRC). The BRC takes full
           responsibility for the judgements that underpin them. We have drawn heavily on the work
           and expertise of numerous officials in preparing these forecasts, including in the Welsh
           Government, Scottish Fiscal Commission, Scottish Government, HM Revenue and Customs
           (HMRC), HM Treasury and the Department for Work and Pensions. We are grateful for their
           expertise, hard work, and patience.

Forecast timetable
1.3        The devolved tax and spending forecasts in this document are consistent with the central
           forecast for the UK economy and public finances presented in our October 2026 EFO.

1.4        The October 2026 EFO describes the timetable that was followed in producing these
           forecasts. The pre-measures economy forecast was closed on 7 September and the pre-
           measures fiscal forecast on 18 September. The forecast was finalised on 22 October after
           incorporating the effects of new policy announcements.

2.1        This chapter presents our latest forecasts for rest of UK taxation and spending, which are

           our forecasts for non-savings non-dividends (NSND) income tax, stamp duty land tax, air

Income tax
2.2        Our forecast for England and Northern Ireland (ENI) NSND income tax forms the basis of
           the income tax BGAs used for budget setting in Scotland and Wales. There are three main
           stages in generating this forecast:

           •      First, we generate a UK-wide forecast for NSND income tax liabilities from the full UK
                  income tax forecast published in our Economic and fiscal outlook (EFO). We set out our
                  latest forecast for UK NSND below in paragraph 2.3;

           •      Second, we calculate the Welsh and Scottish shares of UK-wide NSND liabilities and
                  apply these to the UK forecast. We discuss the Scottish and Welsh shares in more
                  detail in Chapters 3 and 4 respectively.2 This is done on a pre-measures basis, as the
                  effect of measures announced since our previous forecast is instead captured in cash
                  terms. We then subtract the Scottish and Welsh share from our UK NSND forecast to
                  generate an overall ENI forecast; and

           •      Third, we add the effects of UK Government policy measures announced since our
                  previous forecast. 3

ENI share of UK NSND income tax
2.6        Table 2.2 sets out the forecast for the ENI share of UK NSND income tax liabilities on a pre-
           measures basis. We forecast that the ENI share will increase from 90.5 per cent in 2024-25
           to 90.7 per cent by 2031-32. The increase in 2026-27 reflects the reclassification of carried
           interest receipts, which we assume accrue to ENI. We explain this further in Box 2.1.
           Thereafter, the ENI share rises gradually, reflecting differences in projected population
           growth and ageing across the UK. In particular, based on the latest ONS principal
           population projections, Scotland’s population is projected to decline and age more quickly
           than the populations of England and Northern Ireland. We use this to inform the share of
           income tax which accrues to Scotland, based on weighting liabilities by age group. This
           reduces Scotland's share of liabilities and so increases the ENI share.

2.7        Relative to March, the ENI share has been revised up by around 0.1 percentage points
           throughout the forecast period. This is primarily driven by the latest outturn data, which
           point to a lower Scottish share of UK liabilities, which is assumed to persist through the
           forecast. We discuss the drivers of the changes in Welsh and Scottish shares revisions in
           more detail in Chapters 3 and 4.

 Box 2.1: Impact of the reclassification of carried interest on net funding positions
 In October 2024, the UK Government announced that carried interest, the income received by
 fund executives linked to the performance of investment funds, would be taxed as trading profits
 for income tax purposes from April 2026. This reclassification from capital gains tax (CGT) to
 income tax was incorporated in our March 2025 EFO. Prior to April 2026, carried interest was


Devolved tax and spending forecasts                      6
                                                                                 Rest of UK forecasts




subject to CGT, a reserved tax, and therefore had no direct impact on the tax BGAs or devolved
tax revenues. Following its reclassification, carried interest forms part of UK NSND income tax
and therefore affects the Scottish and Welsh net funding positions. In this box, we examine the
impact of the reclassification on those projected net funding positions.
While the OBR does not produce the costings of UK Government policy measures, it is
responsible for scrutinising them and assessing whether they represent reasonable and central
estimates. As part of our role under the devolved fiscal frameworks, this includes assessing the
geographic distribution of policy effects where they affect devolved taxes or the rest of UK
forecasts that underpin the tax BGAs. For carried interest, we judged it reasonable and central to
assume that all additional NSND income tax receipts arising from the reclassification would
accrue to England. This was informed by HMRC analysis of 2022-23 outturn data, which showed
that around 90 per cent of carried interest liabilities were attributable to taxpayers in London and
the South East, with almost all of the remainder arising elsewhere in England. The latest
available data for 2023-24 also shows a similar geographic distribution. On this basis, the
reclassification increases our ENI NSND income tax forecast by around £3.6 billion a year, or
1.1 per cent, from 2026-27 onwards and so impacts the projected indicative income tax BGAs
and net positions of the Welsh and Scottish Governments. Chart A shows that:
   •   For the Scottish income tax net position, the reclassification is projected to increase the
       Scottish income tax BGA by around £250 million a year from 2026-27 onwards and
       therefore reduce the Scottish net position by the same amount. This reflects the
       assumption that the additional NSND income tax receipts accrue entirely to England and
       are therefore included in the rest of UK revenues used to calculate the BGA, while
       generating no corresponding increase in Scottish income tax revenues.
   •   For the Welsh rates net position, the reclassification is projected to have a much smaller
       impact, reducing the net position by around £15 million a year from 2026-27 onwards.
       The proportionately smaller effect relative to Scotland reflects our assumption that the
       majority of carried interest receipts accrue to additional-rate taxpayers in England. Under
       the Welsh fiscal framework, only the first 10p of each income tax band is devolved, while
       the Welsh rates tax BGA is calculated on a by-band basis. Both these factors mean that
       the Welsh net position is much less exposed to changes in NSND income tax liabilities at
       the top of the income distribution in ENI. This means that only a small proportion of the
       increase in ENI NSND income tax receipts arising from the reclassification is projected to
       impact the Welsh rates BGA.

2.9                 Stamp duty land tax (SDLT) receipts in ENI are forecast to increase from £15 billion in
                    2025-26 to £24 billion in 2031-32, an average of 8 per cent annual growth. Relative to
                    March 2026, we have revised down our forecast by an average of £3.7 billion (17 per cent)
                    in each year. This downward revision is because receipts in the first few months of 2026-27
                    imply a lower forecast for both commercial and residential property. This aligns with reports
                    of particularly weak performance in the high-value property market in London and the
                    South East, which receipts are particularly sensitive to given SDLT’s progressive structure. UK
                    Government policy increases SDLT receipts by £0.3 billion in 2028-29, this is more than
                    explained by the measure to restrict sovereign wealth and overseas public pension funds’
                    immunity from direct taxes on UK income and gains. In every other year of the forecast the
                    total effect of new UK Government policy is small.

Introduction
3.1       In this chapter we provide:

          •      A summary of the projections of the tax block grant adjustments (BGAs) and Scottish
                 net tax position. The net tax position reflects the difference between revenues from the
                 Scottish taxes and the tax BGAs. While the BGAs are calculated by HM Treasury, they
                 are based on our forecasts for the equivalent rest of UK taxes set out in Chapter 2.

          •      Our latest forecasts for Scottish taxes, which include Scottish income tax, land and
                 buildings transaction tax (LBTT), Scottish landfill tax, Scottish aggregates tax and air
                 departure tax.

3.2       The rest of UK tax forecasts set out in Chapter 2 are used in calculating the Scottish
          Government’s tax BGAs, which reduce the funding received by the Scottish Government to
          reflect the devolution of tax revenues. They are calculated using the indexed per capita (IPC)
          method, which adjusts the previous year's BGA for each devolved tax in line with the growth
          in the rest of UK tax forecasts and projected differences in population growth. In this
          chapter, to facilitate understanding of how our forecasts inform the tax BGAs, we provide an
          analysis of the latest tax BGAs and how these have changed since they were last calculated.
          However, it is HM Treasury that is responsible for making the formal BGA calculations and
          they are subject to final agreement between the UK Government and Scottish Government.
          The analysis that we set out here should therefore be treated as indicative.

3.3       Our Scottish tax forecasts form the basis of our overall UK receipts forecast. For income tax,
          as described in Chapter 2, our Scottish income tax forecast also informs the calculation of
          the rest of UK forecast which is used to calculate the BGA. However, the forecasts for

3.12                Our Scottish income tax forecast is based on estimating and projecting Scotland's share of

3.13                The Scottish share is forecast to fall from 6.74 per cent in 2024-25 to 6.44 per cent by

                    takes effect in 2026-27, and differences in projected demographic trends across the UK.

                    decline and age more rapidly than elsewhere in the UK. We use an index based on the
                    ONS population projections to reflect the different projected growth rates of the adult
                    population in Scotland compared with the UK as a whole. This ‘population index’ combines
                    two separate indices – one each for adults aged below and above the State Pension age –
                    that are weighted by the proportion of NSND income tax paid by each group. As income tax
                    liabilities are lower for older age groups, this drives a decline in the Scottish share over the

3.16       The Scottish Government’s budget position is based on both the BGAs, which reflect our
           forecasts, and the SFC’s forecasts for tax revenues in Scotland. As a result, differences
           between our respective forecasts for growth in revenues can affect the overall budget
           position in any given year – although ultimately resources are aligned to revenue outturns
           via a reconciliation process over subsequent years.6

3.17       Differences in modelling approaches, data used, and judgements applied can all contribute
           to differences between our and the SFC’s forecasts. This is compounded by the fact that we
           produce our forecasts at different times, so the latest data will have moved on in between
           each forecast including the impact of UK Government or Scottish Government policy
           measures. To facilitate comparisons, as agreed with the SFC, we publish a standard set of
           income tax comparison tables that we each update alongside new forecasts. Tables 3.6 and
           3.7 compare our current forecast to the SFC’s January 2026 forecast.

Introduction
4.1   In this chapter we provide:

      •    A summary of the projections of the Welsh net tax position. The net tax position reflects
           the difference between revenues from the Welsh taxes and the tax block grant
           adjustments (BGAs), which are based on our forecasts for rest of UK taxes set out in
           Chapter 2.

      •    Our latest forecasts for Welsh taxes, which include Welsh rates of income tax, land
           transaction tax (LTT) and landfill disposals tax (LDT).

4.2   Our rest of UK tax forecasts set out in Chapter 2 are used in calculating the Welsh
      Government’s tax BGAs, which reduce the funding received by the Welsh Government to
      reflect the devolution of tax revenues. The tax BGAs in Wales are calculated using the
      ‘comparable model’. This adjusts the previous year’s BGA for each devolved tax in line with
      the per person cash change in the equivalent rest of UK revenues multiplied by a
      comparability factor (which reflects tax revenue per person in Wales as a proportion of
      revenue per person in the rest of the UK at the time of devolution).

4.3   In this chapter, to facilitate understanding of how our forecasts inform the tax BGAs, we
      provide analysis of the latest indicative tax BGAs and how these have changed since they
      were last calculated. However, it is HM Treasury that is responsible for making the formal
      BGA calculations and they are subject to final agreement between the UK Government and
      Welsh Government. The analysis that we set out here should therefore be treated as
      indicative.

4.4   In this chapter we also provide an overview of our latest forecasts for Welsh devolved tax
      revenues. Since 2019, we have provided the official devolved tax forecasts used by the
      Welsh Government for its budget process, which we publish in our Welsh taxes outlook
      (WTO) that accompanies the Welsh draft and final budget. We will be publishing our latest
      WTO, including updated forecasts for Welsh devolved taxes on 17 November to accompany
      the Welsh draft budget. These forecasts also feed into the projections of the Welsh net tax
      position in this chapter.
