October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook
Speaking notes for presentation1 by:
Jonathan Haskel, Chair, Office for Budget Responsibility.



  Fiscal context
        • IMF data shows that in 2006 the UK had net debt of around 40% of
          GDP, close to the average of other advanced economies and well
          below the average of other G7 economies.

        • In the subsequent two decades, the UK’s net debt has risen
          significantly to now be above the average of other G7 countries.

        • And after the rise in borrowing in the pandemic, countries have been
          trying to get borrowing down.

GDP and inflation forecasts
  • The rise in energy prices and interest rates since March has led to a
    deterioration in the near-term economic outlook.

  • This includes weaker GDP growth and higher inflation:

       o In 2027, we now expect GDP growth of around 1%, down from
         about 1½% back in our March forecast.

       o And we anticipate that CPI inflation will be around 3½% rather
         than falling back to 2%.

       o But in 2028 CPI inflation returns to target – about a year later
         than we expected in March.

RHDI
  • As you can see, real household disposable income per person has
    recovered from the fall in the aftermath of the pandemic and 2022
    energy price spike. And, in March, we expected it to grow gradually
    in each year of the forecast.

  • We now expect RHDI per person to fall this year before recovering.
    By 2029-30, a decade after the pandemic, we expect that it will be
    only 1 per cent above pre-pandemic levels.

  • What’s driving that weakness? This is what economists call a
    negative terms-of-trade shock – a way of saying that what we like to
    buy from other countries has got more expensive, so our real
    incomes have fallen.

Fiscal outcomes
  • What does this mean for the forecast path of borrowing?

  • Let me show you the planned paths of borrowing in the last few
    Budgets. And here are the latest plans for public sector net
    borrowing. There are three key points to highlight.

  • First, fiscal consolidation has started. Black lines are outturns and
    show that borrowing has declined from around 5 per cent of GDP
    between 2022-23 and 2024-25, to 4.4 per cent of GDP in 2025-
    26.

  • Second, however, near-term borrowing is more than was expected
    in November 2025. Borrowing between 2026-27 and 2028-29 is
    now forecast to be around 0.5 per cent of GDP higher on average
    than forecast in November 2025.

  • Third, what about the medium term?

       o As you can see from how the green and yellow lines converge,
         the fiscal plans set out in this Parliament all ultimately aimed
         for borrowing to fall to about 2 per cent of GDP by the end of
         the forecasts. The plan set out at this Budget also reaches that
         level by 2029-30 and stays at just below that level to 2031-32.

       o Now, the government has not published detailed spending
         plans for each of those final three years. Instead it sets
         assumptions for the path of overall spending. These
         assumptions, after taking into account areas of protected
         spending such as health, defence and core schools, imply that

Fiscal rules
  • What do these plans then mean for the Government’s performance
    against its fiscal rules?
  • First, we look at the fiscal mandate for the current budget to be in
    surplus in 2029-30:
       o the black line is the forecast path of the current budget.
       o the target year is 29/30 which is the vertical dotted line and the
         red dot is at zero in that year.
       o so this shows you the fiscal rule is met on the central forecast
         and this gap (between dot and black line in 29/30) is £15bn.
  • Now for the supplementary rule – through which the government
    must ensure that Public Sector Net Financial Liabilities is falling in
    2029-30:
       o The dot is the previous year’s figure and the gap shows it’s
         falling by £24bn.
  • These are small margins – with the one against the fiscal mandate
    being below the average post-pandemic revision in pre-measures
    borrowing in the third year of the forecast. And there remains
    significant uncertainty, as we show through these fan charts.
  • As you can see, the bands are wide which show that outcomes
    significantly higher or lower than our central forecast are quite likely.
    The probability of meeting the fiscal mandate is now 56% and the

Risks
  • Most of this presentation relates to our central forecast but there are
    significant risks and uncertainties around this which we discuss
    extensively in the EFO.
  • In terms of economic and fiscal risks, we have the following:
        o Economic: energy prices, productivity growth, labour market
          outlook
        o Fiscal: debt servicing costs, departmental spending, welfare
          spending.

Summary
  • Challenging fiscal background…
        o Government debt at elevated levels
        o Since March, higher energy prices and interest rates
  • Economic forecast:
        o 2027 GDP growth lowered to 1%
        o 2027 CPI inflation higher at 3½%
        o But inflation falls and growth recovers in medium-term
  • Fiscal forecast:
        o Near-term pace of consolidation slows
        o Fiscal rules forecast to be met in 2029-30: Current budget in
          surplus by £15bn, with probability of 56%; PSNFL falling by
          £24bn, with probability of 54%.
        o Plans would stabilise debt but still at elevated levels
        o Wide range of upside and downside risks
