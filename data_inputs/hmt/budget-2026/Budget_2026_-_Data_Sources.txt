Preface
This document provides details of the data sources for the text, charts,
and tables in the Budget 2026 document. It also includes further detail
on sources where information additional to that provided in footnotes
may be helpful to users and, where necessary, how they have been
calculated.

This document ensures that the Treasury meets the standards set out
by the UK Statistics Authority to comply with the Statistics and
Registration Act 20071. In particular, this document aims to meet the
principles of the UK Statistics Authority’s ‘Code of Practice for Statistics2’
with regard to Trustworthiness, Quality and Value, and which includes
standards for Intelligent Transparency3. It also aims to provide an
example of the voluntary application of this code to outputs that are
not official statistics.
The main standards that this document addresses are in the Standards
for the Public Use of Statistics, Data and Wider Analysis, under
Supporting Understanding and Equality of Access, that departments
should:
        •    “Be clear about where statistics, data and wider analysis used in
             public communications come from.”
        •    “Clearly communicate any key context and limitations
             associated with statistics, data and wider analysis to help the
             public understand and interpret them.”
        •    “Publish statistics, data and wider analysis separately from any
             related policy and ministerial statements”.
        •    “Where possible, draw on the latest and most reliable published
             official statistics.”
It does this by:
•      Distinguishing clearly between outturn data that are official
       statistics, and other data and estimates that are not official
       statistics4. Examples of estimates that are not official statistics
       include forecasts, management information and economic

Fuller details of sources, for example ONS CDIDs, are in the main only
provided for outturn data up to 2025-26. Data for future years are
forecasts, which are outside the domain of official statistics, although
some are within the definition of wider analysis. Estimates of the
impacts and costings of government policy decisions fall into the same
category, as these are estimated, rather than measured or compiled
using statistical processes.
In many cases, data for 2026-27 onwards are taken from the Office for
Budget Responsibility’s (OBR) October 2026 ‘Economic and Fiscal
Outlook’ (EFO)7. Where this is the case, it is mentioned in the text or in
this document.
Time series for data that are shown with Office for National Statistics
(ONS) time series identifier codes can be downloaded from the ONS
website at: https://www.ons.gov.uk/

Chapter 3
Paragraph 3.20
Data
Number of carers expected to benefit in England and Wales
Data source
DWP estimates
Further reference information
In 2030/31, around 48,000 people are estimated to benefit as a result of
the measure, including people who become newly entitled under the
proposed taper. Of these, around 3,000 are estimated to live in Wales.
The overall estimate is calculated by comparing modelled awards
under the proposed taper, with awards under the existing Carer’s
Allowance earnings limit. The Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings
(ASHE) is used to estimate the weekly earning distribution of
employees in England and Wales. The Family Resource Survey (FRS) is
used to estimate the characteristics of full-time carers, including sex,
industry, occupation and full-time or part-time employment status. FRS
characteristic estimates are used to reweight the estimated ASHE
distribution of weekly earnings, to produce an estimate of earnings for
current and future Carer’s Allowance recipients.
Estimation of beneficiaries in Wales is calculated by first segmenting
the Welsh share of the total England and Wales Carer’s Allowance in-
payment group and recalculating a Carer’s Allowance earnings
distribution for Wales.
Both estimates are sensitive to assumptions about earnings, take-up
and carer's behavioural responses.

The approach taken to the calculation is as follows:

   •   Cities in each country were ranked based on their number of
       employees. Average productivity for the ten largest cities outside
       the lead city was compared to productivity in the lead city for
       each country. The lead city was selected as the largest city in
       France (Paris), Italy (Milan) and the UK (London). In Germany
       Munich was chosen as the leading city despite Berlin and the
       Ruhrgebiet having a higher number of employees. This approach
       is consistent with Centre For Cities (2024)

   •   The gap in productivity levels between large cities to leading city
       in England was -35%. The average of the productivity gaps for
       France (-25%), Germany (-21%), and Italy (-23%) is -23%.
   •   Productivity across the ten largest UK cities outside London were
       then increased to reduce the productivity gap to London from –
       35% to –23%. The resulting percentage change in UK GDP is then
       reweighted to an England basis using the ratio of England to UK
